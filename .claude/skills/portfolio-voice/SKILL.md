# The voice

This is not a style guide. A list of rules produces prose that reads like a checklist being completed. What follows is a description of how one person writes, drawn from the prose already on the site.

**What that corpus actually is**, corrected 2026-08-21 in task `024210826` by measuring the `translations` table instead of quoting a file: **58 English captions and 25 Arabic** · **36 English page bodies and 22 Arabic** · 248 English chapter paragraphs and 139 Arabic · 41 cover paragraphs · 20 decision bodies · 12 entry handles. An earlier draft of this skill said *140 captions and 22 pages*; the 140 came from a Cloudinary inventory file and counts **tags, not captions**. The claim mattered because the moves below are inferences from repetition, and an inference is only as wide as the corpus under it. Every figure quoted from here on is measured, and the corpus it was measured on is named.

Write as that person. When a sentence could go two ways, ask which one he would have written, not which one satisfies a rule.

---

## Who is writing

A designer of fifteen years who has spent the last four inside a regulated bank, and who is describing work most of the people reading it will never see. He is not selling. He is explaining what he found, what he decided, and what it cost.

He assumes the reader is intelligent and busy. He does not warm them up. He does not tell them what he is about to tell them.

He concedes things nobody asked him to concede. This is not modesty — it is what makes the rest of it believable.

He knows the domain deeply enough to be specific, and specific enough that a banker reading it would recognise the problem instantly. Emirates ID NFC, maker–checker, FATCA branches, the commercial register that arrives as one page. He never explains a term the reader in his head would already know.

## The moves that recur

These are observed, not prescribed. They appear again and again because they are how he thinks.

### He defines by negation — and where he does it matters more than that he does

**A caption move, not a house move.** Measured across the whole site in task `024210826`, per 100 words:

| Where | rate | |
|---|---|---|
| **captions** | **1.15** | 21% of captions carry one — its home |
| page bodies (About, Philosophy, Systems, Contact) | 0.77 | |
| chapter paragraphs | 0.70 | |
| decision bodies | 0.58 | |
| cover paragraphs | 0.37 | |
| chapter context and result | 0.28 | |
| **case-file thesis, role, reflection** | **0.24** | a fifth of the caption rate |
| **entry-handle payoffs** | **0.00** | he never does it here |

**So: dense in a caption, rare in anything that opens a case file, absent from a handle.** That is the part to hold on to. Knowing the move exists makes an agent write it everywhere; knowing where he does not use it is what keeps the prose his.

*(An earlier draft called this "the single most recognisable move in the entire body of work" and put it at "roughly two in five captions". Two in five — 39.7% — is the rate at which captions contain **any** negation word, which is a different and much weaker claim. The construction itself is at 21%.)*

> The trail — **not a log of failures, but** a record of the conversation the bank used to have by phone and never wrote down.

> the classification **is not** reporting metadata. **It is** the address the replacement will land at.

> The journey removes re-typing, **not** the human.

> the work follows the map, **not** the org chart.

> Rescheduling as a recorded event, **not** a gap in the timeline.

> Resolved the moment the customer is done — **not** when the bank agrees.

It works because it kills the obvious reading first. The reader was about to think *audit log*, and the sentence takes that away before they settle into it.

Do not use it in every paragraph. It loses its force by the third.

### He opens with a definite article, as though the reader is already there

> **The** page the officer fills on the customer's behalf.

> **The** queue an officer opens each morning.

> **The** artefact the whole journey replaces.

> **The** door, found.

Not "a screen showing…", not "this is the queue where…". The definite article assumes a shared frame. It puts the reader inside the room rather than in front of a slide.

**The opener can be shorter than the sentence that follows it, and often is.** Seen whole, both of these open on a fragment and then run long:

> **The door, found.** An applicant who wants out and cannot leave stays in the system as a ghost.

> **الباب، وقد وُجد.** من يريد الخروج ولا يستطيع يبقى في النظام سجلًا معلقًا.

Short first, long second. This is the shape — not its mirror.

### He anchors abstraction in a physical object

Every time the writing risks becoming a concept, an object arrives:

> the folder on a desk, replaced by a list that knows how long it has been waiting

> eighteen pages, filled by hand, then typed again by someone else

> a day of appointments, each one a room they have to be in

> from the moment the pen leaves the paper

> the phone call that used to depend on whether someone remembered to make it

The object is doing the argument. "Reduced operational friction" says nothing. "The folder on a desk" says all of it.

### Numbers live inside sentences

Never a statistic set apart, never a metric in a box.

> **eight of ten** could not define the terms it asks them to certify

> Why **nine days** were **nine days**

> **One button became six** — every way a visit can actually end, named and recordable

> Why **seven of ten** could leave and come back

A number in this voice is always doing narrative work. If it can be lifted out and put on a card, it is in the wrong place.

### He concedes inside the sentence

> **Not what I asked for, and defensible.**

> The compromise that shipped

> And I lost Arabic-first permanently — correctly, I now think

> The deliberate rejection path

The concession is never a disclaimer at the end. It sits in the middle of the claim, which is what stops the claim reading as a pitch.

### The short line is an entry-handle format, not a closing habit

**Deleted as a general move, 2026-08-21, task `024210826`, because it does not survive measurement.** An earlier draft of this skill said *"he closes short"* on the strength of four examples. Across the site the opposite is true — the last sentence of a paragraph is the **longest** one in it, two times in three:

| Corpus | earlier sentences | last sentence | last is longer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **entry-handle payoffs** | 17.3 words | **6.3** | 17% |
| captions | 11.4 | 10.8 | 46% |
| page bodies | 15.5 | 17.2 | 52% |
| decision bodies | 15.3 | 18.5 | 55% |
| cover paragraphs | 14.1 | 17.5 | 59% |
| **chapter paragraphs** | 12.5 | **17.4** | **67%** |

**The one place it is real is the entry-handle payoff:** 6.3 words against 17.3 for the sentence before it, and 75% end in six words or fewer. That is a **fixed shape for that field** — a handle has to land in one line — not a habit of his that can be carried into a chapter.

**Do not force a short final sentence.** Write until the thought ends. If it ends short, it ends short; the unevenness below is what does the work, and manufacturing a clipped last line is one of the more recognisable ways of faking it.

*(Two of that draft's examples — `The door, found.` and `الباب، وقد وُجد.` — are not closes at all. Both are the **first** sentence of a two-sentence caption, followed by a longer one. They belong under the definite-article opening above, and that is where they now sit.)*

## What this voice does not do

**It does not use adjectives to carry weight.** Read fifty of his captions and count them — there are almost none. *Seamless*, *robust*, *intuitive*, *powerful*, *cutting-edge* do not appear. The nouns and verbs do the work. If a sentence needs an adjective to be impressive, the sentence is wrong.

**It does not hedge.** No *arguably*, no *it could be said*, no *in many ways*. When he is uncertain he says so directly — *I now think*, *and defensible* — which is different from hedging.

**It does not announce structure.** No *firstly*, no *in this section we will*, no *to summarise*. The paragraph starts where the thought starts.

**It does not use the vocabulary of design marketing.** *User-centric*, *journey mapping*, *stakeholder alignment*, *leveraged*, *drove* — none of it. He describes what happened. The methodology is visible in the description without being named.

**It does not say "we" for work he did.** Every claim is first person or it is attributed. This is the single hardest rule on the site and it is load-bearing.

**It does not put a metric anywhere without its marker.** `[achieved]` `[projected]` `[not-measurable]` — see the metric-integrity skill. A number with no marker is not a style problem, it is a refusal condition.

## The em dash, and what the rule actually is

His writing is full of em dashes and they work. A blanket ban on them stood in `docs/learn.md` and `docs/content-brief.md` until 2026-08-21, and it broke sentences that were already right. **It was measured and it does not survive measurement:** 53 of 58 English captions carry a dash, at 4.79 per 100 words.

**This is now the only place the rule lives.** `docs/learn.md` points here; the copies in `docs/content-brief.md` and `.claude/agents/content.md` were deleted in task `024210826` and replaced with pointers, because three copies of one rule is how three versions of it appear.

**One rule, both languages. There is no Arabic exception** — see *Arabic*, below.

The real distinction:

**His dash pivots.** It turns from the object to what the object means, and the two halves do different work:

> Name screening, brought inside. This used to arrive as a PDF from a separate system — readable, but nothing could be done to it.

**The machine dash balances.** Two clauses of similar length and similar shape, the dash holding them in symmetry, neither one earning its place:

> The system was designed for clarity — a principle that guided every decision we made.

Test each dash: does the second half say something the first half could not? If it is restating, decorating, or balancing, cut it. If it is turning, keep it.

The same test applies to the rhythm generally. AI prose is recognisable by its evenness — clauses of matching length, sentences of matching shape, paragraphs that all land the same way. His prose is uneven on purpose. A twenty-word sentence, then four words.

## Arabic

The Arabic is an original text, not a translation. It is read by an Arabic-speaking recruiter or curator as the primary document.

The same moves carry, in Arabic's own constructions:

**Negation-definition** becomes `ليس … بل`:

> السجل — **ليس** سجل إخفاقات، **بل** محضر المحادثة التي كان البنك يجريها هاتفيًا ولا يكتبها.

> مسار الرفض المقصود — الشركة غير المؤهلة تُسلّم إلى مدير علاقة، **لا** إلى طريق مسدود.

**The fragment opener** finds an Arabic form rather than a translated one:

> **الباب، وقد وُجد.** من يريد الخروج ولا يستطيع يبقى في النظام سجلًا معلقًا.

That is not a rendering of *The door, found*. It is the Arabic way of doing the same thing, and it is better in Arabic than a literal version would be. **Note where it sits: it opens, and the long sentence follows it.** An earlier draft filed this under a closing move; the measurement in task `024210826` found it is the first sentence, exactly as its English counterpart is.

**The concession:**

> **ليس ما طلبته، وقابل للدفاع.**

### Arabic conventions, fixed

- Technical and brand terms stay in Latin script inside Arabic text: `KYC` `OTP` `RTL` `NDA` `WCAG` `IoT` `FATCA` `Flex` `LinkedIn` `Mashreq` `Cervello`.
- Numerals are Western: `2024`, `1,500+`.
- `نيوبيزنس` in Arabic; `Neobiz` in Latin.
- `accessibility` → `قابلية الوصول والاستخدام`.
- `exception` → `الاستفسار`, not `الاستثناء`. In this system an exception is a directed query, not an anomaly.
- **The accusative carries its tanween** — object, `حال` or adverb alike, not adverbs only. `شيئًا` `منتجًا` `أحدًا` `مكتوبًا` `دفعةً` `سجلًا معلقًا` as much as `نيابةً` `شفاهةً` `لاحقًا` `بنيويًا`. Corrected in task `024210826`: the rule used to read *adverbial accusatives*, and the measurement found the practice is wider and simpler than that — the most frequent marked tokens on the site are objects and `حال`, not adverbs.
- **The tanween sits before the alif: `ًا`, not `اً`.** That is both the majority form on the site (448 against 92) and the typographically correct one. **The rule is written here; the existing text is not to be normalised in passing.** Every one of the 92 exceptions is in `page_section.body` — the static pages, which were written in a different context, not under a different convention. Fixing them is a content task with its own brief, and a sweep run as a side effect of some other work is exactly how a real distinction gets flattened.
- Punctuation is Arabic: `،` `؛` `؟`.
- **The em dash is not treated differently in Arabic.** The claim that it is foreign to Arabic punctuation and should be avoided stood in three files and is false about this site's own text: measured in task `024210826`, the Arabic uses it **more** per word than the English does, in every corpus that has one — 5.97 per 100 words in captions against the English 4.79, 2.04 in chapter prose against 1.58. **The pivot-versus-balance test above is the whole rule, in both languages.**

### What "original, not translated" means in practice

The English explains the word `تصميم` to a foreign reader. The Arabic cannot do that — it would be defining the reader's own word to him. So the Arabic makes a different point in the same place.

The Philosophy page adds `الإعلان` to a list the English renders as graphics and illustration, because in the Arabic professional context the confusion comes from advertising first. That was flagged, approved, and kept.

Where the two languages must say the same thing — a claim, a number, a caveat — they say the same thing. Where they are explaining, each explains to its own reader.

## Before writing anything new

**In force from 2026-08-21, task `024210826`. See decision 056.**

New prose is not written from general knowledge. It is written from an interview.

**The interview runs with Moataz, in the main session, and never inside a subagent.** Grilling is a long back-and-forth: ask the frontier, wait, let the answers reshape the tree, ask again, until the frontier is empty. A subagent gets **one** reply and its turn ends — it cannot hold a conversation, so it cannot hold this one. So:

- **The orchestrator runs the session** with Moataz directly, and does not act on it until he confirms the understanding is shared.
- **The content agent writes from the answers of a session that has already finished**, relayed in its brief. Those answers are its source material, and its only one.
- **Briefed to write new prose with no answers to write from, the content agent stops and asks for them.** It does not open an interview of its own, and it does not proceed on general knowledge. That is `BLOCKED`, with the request returned to the orchestrator.

This is not a preference — the one case file written by reconstructing an old presentation instead produced eighteen passages presenting inference as Moataz's own decisions, and they are still awaiting his fact-check.

If a fact is needed and the interview did not produce it, that is a gap to report, not a gap to fill.

## The test

Read the draft and ask:

1. Could a competent designer at any bank have written this sentence? If yes, it is not specific enough.
2. Is there a physical object anywhere in this paragraph? If not, the abstraction has nothing holding it down.
3. Does every dash turn, or is one of them balancing?
4. Are the sentences all about the same length?
5. Is there a claim here with no concession anywhere near it?
6. Would a banker reading this recognise the problem in the first line?
7. If this is a thesis, a role statement, a cover paragraph or an entry handle — is there a *not X, but Y* in it? He almost never puts one there. That construction belongs to captions.
8. Did the last sentence come out short because the thought ended, or because a short last sentence felt like the voice? Only the first is.

The failure mode is not bad writing. It is writing that is perfectly competent and belongs to nobody.
